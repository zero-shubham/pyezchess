# Level 1 — Fundamentals & Mechanics

Curriculum content for Level 1. Referenced by `get_level_details` when Vishy teaches this level.

---

## Topic 1.1 — The Battlefield

**Core concepts**: Board orientation, ranks (horizontal rows 1–8), files (vertical columns a–h), diagonals. The "white on right" rule — the bottom-right corner square (h1 for White) must always be a light square.

**Teaching guidelines**: Use concrete square names (e.g. e4, d5). Walk the student through naming squares and identifying ranks, files, and diagonals. Ask them to identify squares to confirm understanding.

**Common mistakes**: Confusing ranks and files; forgetting which corner should be light.

**Example positions**: Start from the standard starting position FEN to practice square identification.

## Topic 1.2 — The Army

**Core concepts**: All six piece types, their movement patterns, and how capturing works. Emphasize the Knight's unique L-shape movement (two squares in one direction, one square perpendicular) and its ability to jump over other pieces. All other pieces cannot jump.

**Teaching guidelines**: Introduce pieces one at a time. Use example squares to demonstrate movement range and capture rules.

**Common mistakes**: Thinking the Knight moves in a "Z" instead of an "L"; forgetting that pieces (except Knight) cannot jump over others.

**Example positions**: Place a Knight on f3 and ask the student to name all squares it can reach.

## Topic 1.3a — Special Rules: Castling

**Core concepts**: Castling as the "King's shield." Kingside (short) castling: King goes to g1, Rook goes to f1. Queenside (long) castling: King goes to c1, Rook goes to d1. Requirements: neither the King nor the chosen Rook has previously moved; no pieces between them; the King is not in check; the King does not pass through or land on an attacked square.

**Teaching guidelines**: Explain the strategic value first (King safety + Rook activation), then walk through the four requirements. Use concrete square names throughout.

**Common mistakes**: Trying to castle after the King or Rook has moved; attempting to castle through check; forgetting that the King cannot pass through attacked squares.

**Example positions**: Set up positions where castling is legal and illegal to test each requirement.

## Topic 1.3b — Special Rules: En Passant

**Core concepts**: If a pawn advances two squares from its starting position and lands beside an enemy pawn, the enemy pawn may capture it as if it had only moved one square, diagonally behind it. This capture must be made immediately on the next move or the opportunity is lost forever.

**Teaching guidelines**: Emphasize the "now or never" nature. Walk through a concrete example — e.g. White plays d2–d4, landing beside Black's e4 pawn; Black can capture with e4xd3.

**Common mistakes**: Missing the en passant window; thinking the capture works on any pawn advance (not just two squares from starting rank).

**Example positions**: Create a position where White just pushed d2–d4 and Black has a pawn on e4; demonstrate the en passant capture on d3.

---

## Topic 1.3c — Special Rules: Pawn Promotion

**Core concepts**: When a pawn reaches the opposite end of the board (8th rank for White, 1st rank for Black) it must be promoted to another piece. Promotion options: Queen, Rook, Bishop, or Knight. Promoting to a Queen is most common, but promoting to a Knight (underpromotion) is sometimes tactically useful.

**Teaching guidelines**: Explain why promotion is powerful. Give a brief example of underpromotion, e.g. promoting to a Knight to simultaneously fork the enemy King and Queen.

**Common mistakes**: Not knowing promotion is mandatory; always promoting to Queen without considering tactical underpromotion.

**Example positions**: Show a pawn on the 7th rank about to promote; set up a position where underpromotion to a Knight delivers a decisive fork.

## Topic 1.4 — The Endgame Goal

**Core concepts**: Check — the King is under direct attack and must escape. Checkmate — the King is in check with no legal escape; the game ends immediately. No-winner scenarios: Stalemate (player to move has no legal moves but is NOT in check — draw), and other draws including insufficient material, threefold repetition, the 50-move rule, and mutual agreement.

**Teaching guidelines**: Distinguish checkmate from stalemate carefully — beginners commonly confuse the two. Use simple examples for each.

**Common mistakes**: Confusing stalemate with checkmate; not realizing the game is a draw in stalemate.

**Example positions**: Show a simple back-rank checkmate; show a trapped King with no legal moves but not in check (stalemate).

## Topic 1.5 — Material Valuation

**Core concepts**: Standard point values — Pawn = 1, Knight = 3, Bishop = 3, Rook = 5, Queen = 9. The King cannot be traded. Evaluate trades by comparing points exchanged: giving up more than you gain is a losing trade (blunder); gaining more than you give up wins material.

**Teaching guidelines**: Use simple trade scenarios and ask the student to evaluate them before revealing the answer, e.g. "If I give up my Rook for your Bishop, who comes out ahead?"

**Common mistakes**: Making trades without counting points; trading a Queen for a Knight without realizing the material loss.

**Example positions**: Set up positions with potential captures and ask the student to calculate whether the trade favors them.

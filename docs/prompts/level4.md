# Level 4 — Intermediate Strategy & Endgame Mastery

Curriculum content for Level 4. Referenced by `get_level_details` when Vishy teaches this level.

---

## Topic 4.1 — Pawn Anatomy

**Core concepts**: Passed pawn — a pawn with no enemy pawns on the same or adjacent files in front of it, making it a candidate for promotion. Doubled pawns — two pawns of the same color on the same file; they are generally weak because they cannot protect each other and block each other's path. Pawn islands — groups of pawns separated by open files; fewer islands mean a more compact, easier-to-defend pawn structure.

**Teaching guidelines**: Show concrete examples of each pawn structure. Explain the strategic implications: passed pawns are assets to push; doubled pawns create weak squares and reduce flexibility; fewer pawn islands mean safer endgames. Ask the student to count pawn islands and identify passed pawns in example positions.

**Common mistakes**: Ignoring pawn structure entirely; creating doubled pawns without compensation; not pushing a passed pawn in the endgame.

**Example positions**: A position with a clear passed pawn on the a-file; a position with doubled c-pawns showing the weak d4/d5 squares; a position with three pawn islands vs two.

## Topic 4.2 — Piece Coordination

**Core concepts**: The Bishop Pair — two Bishops working together control both color complexes and are especially powerful in open positions. Rooks on open files — placing Rooks on files with no pawns maximizes their mobility and allows them to penetrate the 7th rank. Coordinated pieces support each other and multiply their effectiveness.

**Teaching guidelines**: Demonstrate how the Bishop Pair dominates in open positions where pawns do not block diagonals. Show how doubling Rooks on an open file creates severe pressure. Ask the student to identify the best square for a Rook or how to preserve the Bishop Pair.

**Common mistakes**: Trading one Bishop for a Knight unnecessarily and losing the Bishop Pair advantage; placing Rooks on closed files where they have no scope.

**Example positions**: Open position where the Bishop Pair controls long diagonals; position with an open e-file where doubling Rooks creates a decisive advantage.

## Topic 4.3 — The Active King

**Core concepts**: In the middlegame the King must be protected. In the endgame, the King transforms from a liability into a powerful attacking piece. King activation means using the King to support pawn advances, attack enemy pawns, and control key squares. The transition occurs when there are few pieces left and the risk of checkmate is minimal.

**Teaching guidelines**: Contrast a middlegame position (King safely castled) with an endgame position (King marching to the center). Explain that "king in the center" is a weakness in the opening but a strength in the endgame. Show when and how to bring the King forward.

**Common mistakes**: Keeping the King permanently on the back rank in the endgame; bringing the King out too early while enemy pieces still pose mating threats.

**Example positions**: King and pawn endgame where the active King escorts a passed pawn to promotion; a position where the King is needed in the center to control the opposition.

## Topic 4.4 — Basic King & Pawn Endings

**Core concepts**: Opposition — the chess version of a staring contest, where two Kings face each other with one square between them. The side not to move has the opposition and controls the advance. Using opposition to escort a pawn to promotion is a fundamental endgame technique. Key rule: the King must be in front of its pawn to promote successfully.

**Teaching guidelines**: Set up simple King + pawn vs King positions. Demonstrate opposition by moving the Kings toward each other. Show how the attacking King uses opposition to force a path for the pawn. Ask the student to find the winning move in opposition positions.

**Common mistakes**: Pushing the pawn too early and losing the opposition; not using the King to support the pawn; not understanding that the defending side wants to gain the opposition to block the pawn.

**Example positions**: King on e4, enemy King on e6, White pawn on e3 — demonstrate how White gains opposition and advances; a position where losing opposition turns a win into a draw.

## Topic 4.5 — Planning

**Core concepts**: Planning in chess means forming a long-term idea instead of reacting move by move. A key planning question: "What is my worst-placed piece?" Improving the worst-placed piece is often the best plan. Other planning elements: identifying pawn breaks, finding the right exchange, and choosing which side of the board to play on.

**Teaching guidelines**: Present middlegame positions and ask the student to identify the worst-placed piece and how to improve it. Walk through a sample plan: reposition a bad Bishop, prepare a pawn break, or transfer a Knight to a better outpost. Reinforce that plans should be flexible and adapt to the opponent's moves.

**Common mistakes**: Making random moves without a plan; focusing only on tactical shots and ignoring strategic improvement; sticking to a plan even when the position has changed.

**Example positions**: A position where a Bishop is blocked by its own pawns and should be re-routed; a position where a Rook is trapped on a closed file and needs to be transferred to an open one.
